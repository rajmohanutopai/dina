Core cannot honour or verify a stock reservation, and modelling one would put a commercial commitment inside the kernel.   3. Unit vocabulary v1 is a closed list; custom units are rejected (spec §27 Q4, now closed).   4. This is both smaller and safer than the spec-faithful reading, and it removes the "validated counters" failure mode entirely.

Architecture decision — why a refactor, not more patches

Four review rounds kept finding second-order breaks: a correct, tested fix in one place broke a rule enforced in another. Twice the damage came from my own remediation — the §11.2 ownership gate silently disabled §16.2 disaster recovery, and persisting valid_until supplied the last ingredient for reviving an expired pre-backup quote family. That is not bad luck four times; it is a structural signal.

The smells, measured:

Named: shotgun surgery (one idea, many edit sites — the cost of a change tracks how many sites you remember, and I remembered three of four); anemic domain model (rows plus discipline, so a quote family cannot refuse an illegal operation because it is not a thing); lying casts (the compiler told not to look at exactly the boundary that needed checking); and a non-exhaustive switch over parsed JSON (the type system asserting a fact about untrusted data). The tests were the only net holding these together, and seven of them turned out to hold nothing.

Recorded because I got the reasoning wrong first: I declined the deeper fix partly because it was a day’s work and would invalidate four review rounds. Neither is a reason. Reviews are cheap to re-run; wrong structure is not cheap to live with. The only legitimate constraint is avoiding rigidity.

Agreed design (refined against a Codex review of the proposal)

Plain validated data crosses boundaries. Pure domain aggregates own local state rules. Transactional application services coordinate cross-aggregate changes. Raw persistence primitives are not available to production callers.

Four corrections to my first proposal, all accepted:

  • Uniqueness across (buyer_did, purchase_order_id) and (buyer_did, idempotency_key) is a database invariant. One CommerceOrder cannot own a rule about all orders.
  • Deciding an order and settling its quote hold spans two aggregates plus receipts. It belongs to a transactional application service, not to CommerceOrder — my version would have pushed quote knowledge into the order.
  • Typed results, not throws, for business outcomes. Expired, consumed, superseded and illegal-transition are normal results that travel to the buyer as wire reason_codes. Corruption and impossible state (a failed settlement inside a committed decision; a head row whose receipt is missing) throw and roll back. Conflating the two is arguably what produced the ignored settleUse boolean.
  • Hiding the repositories is the whole point. QuoteFamily.advance() is worthless while casAdvanceHead() stays callable — the next call site simply bypasses it. Success is measured by a grep returning empty, not by a passing test.

One discipline added on top: application services coordinate and persist, and must not re-derive aggregate rules. A service that re-checks epoch or capacity “to be safe” has scattered the rule again and become a god-object with extra steps.

Wire types stay plain serialisable data with validators — they are hashed byte-exactly, frozen in conformance vectors, and must be reproducible by a Go/Rust/Swift port. Turning them into classes would fight both. No unit-of-work ceremony over the existing synchronous DatabaseAdapter and explicit TxRunner: that is rigidity, not correctness.

Decisions worth knowing:

  • Refusals are values; corruption throws. Expired, consumed, superseded, voided, stale-epoch and duplicate are ordinary outcomes that become wire reason_codes, so they are returned. A settlement CAS that cannot fail failing, or a head row that will not read back after being written, throws — those mean the ledger and the order references disagree, and committing half a decision is worse than aborting.
  • The aggregate does not re-check the protocol verifier. Revision numbering, audience, request binding, in-chain epoch regression and immutable max_uses belong to verifyQuoteRevisionExtends. My first version re-implemented two of them locally — the same scattering, one layer down — and answered with a vaguer message than the real one. It now calls the verifier and carries its sentence through in a detail field. Only one rule is the aggregate's alone: epoch monotonicity, which compares the head to Core's restore history and is invisible to a verifier that only sees two revisions.
  • detail is operator text, never wire text. The buyer-facing reason_code is derived from the refusal name alone, through a total Record<QuoteRefusal, string> — so a new refusal cannot reach a counterparty unhandled. stale_epoch maps to quote_voided: to the buyer they mean the same thing, this family is gone.
  • Duplicate is decided before capacity. Capacity-first told a caller retrying its own order that the quote was consumed — by the caller's own hold.
  • hold() checks expiry itself rather than trusting that admits() ran first. An aggregate whose rules only hold when methods are called in the right order is the discipline this refactor exists to remove. It judges against the head's window, which the family keeps current; admits() judges the record the order names, which may be an honoured superseded revision with a different deadline.
  • An instance is a one-transaction snapshot of the head row. Stated in the class doc because nothing in the type system says so.

Debt this leaves, recorded rather than hidden: the restore sweep still calls voidUnexpired, which by construction cannot see an already-expired family. It is kept because the restore is an event that must leave a receipt with a real count — the fence itself is now epoch monotonicity, which needs no sweep and cannot miss a row.

WS-0.3 — StatusChain aggregate (done)

packages/core/src/commerce/status_chain.ts. Chain CREATION, ADVANCEMENT and FENCING are now three operations on one object, and CommerceStatusHeadRepository is unreachable from production. Each operation carries its own restore prerequisite: create refuses when the ORDER predates the epoch, advance refuses when the HEAD does, and fence requires a higher epoch because fencing is how a restored chain rejoins.

The rule this closes escaped three times. Missing from signStatusUpdate; then missing from signStatusUpdateInTx once added to the first; then, shared as a predicate, unable to express chain creation at all — the predicate takes a head epoch, and at genesis there is no head. That last one is worth stating plainly, because I got it wrong myself and published a table asserting genesis was safe: the state that matters after a restore is the BUYER's copy, not the local row. A restored supplier whose head row was lost signs a second sequence-0 record, the buyer holds two, and the fence cannot repair it — unavailable before a genesis, blocked afterwards by the fork check. Local absence is the restore symptom, never proof of safety.

WS-0.6 — authorization separated from existence at the ingress gate (done)

The §11.2 gate treated all three order-scoped capabilities identically: prove you own an existing order reference, or be refused. That was right for two of them and broke the third.

The defect, which was mine. order_reconcile exists to resolve outcome_unknown (§12.7/§16.2): the buyer submitted an order and never learned whether it landed. The case that matters most is the one where the supplier holds NO reference — it crashed before the durable write, or restored a backup taken before the order arrived. Requiring an existing reference made exactly that case unanswerable. My own §11.2 remediation silently disabled the disaster recovery it sat next to.

The fix is a distinction, not a bypass. Two capabilities are entitlement-by-possession (order_status, cancel_order): you may ask about an order you own, so the reference IS the proof and absence stays a denial. One is entitlement-by-evidence (order_reconcile): authorization comes from the buyer-bound payload, which must name the authenticated sender, and whether a reference exists is the handler's answer rather than the gate's business.

Why this leaks nothing. A reconcile for someone else's order is keyed on the authenticated sender and also answers never_received, so "no such order" and "not your order" stay indistinguishable — the endpoint is not an existence oracle. That is exactly why the same relaxation would be wrong for order_status, and a test now pins that contrast rather than leaving it as reasoning in a comment.

WS-4.2 — the restore fence had no trigger, and I had just made it reachable (part)

Say the bad part first: my own WS-2.4 change opened this. The backup's use counters would be live again: capacity already spent, spendable a second time. Why nothing caught it. establishAfterRestore() — which increments the epoch, voids unexpired capacity, and writes the fence receipt — is fully implemented and well tested, and had no caller anywhere. The same shape as the engines and the ingress bridge: correct code, reachable by nobody.

Written in the import's OWN transaction. Inside the same transaction, restored capacity and the obligation to void it are one atomic fact.

The marker is set unconditionally, with no attempt to detect whether a given archive carried commerce rows: an empty commerce set costs one wasted epoch increment, a missed one lets capacity be spent twice. WS-3.4 — the extension-operation broker (part)

The problem §3.4 solves. A plugin runner is untrusted code out of process that must be able to ask for effects only Dina can perform — search the AppView, send a D2D message — without ever holding the authority to perform them. The answer is a four-step protocol, and this is its state machine: propose, permit, execute, verify.

Durable first, because every step boundary is a crash boundary. The proposal is recorded before anything is permitted, so a crash leaves a readable row rather than an intention that evaporated. executing is written BEFORE the effect, so a crash mid-effect is discoverable through listExecuting() instead of being retried into a double send. That is also why outcome_unknown is a first-class terminal state rather than an error: an effect that MAY have happened is a different fact from one that certainly did not, and only the first forbids a retry.

Two orderings that are load-bearing. Deny-before-validation: an operation the capability never declared is refused without consulting the registry or touching params — those params are not input this node should be parsing. And params are validated BEFORE the row exists, because a recorded proposal is something the owner may be asked to permit, and it should never be possible to be asked about params nobody checked.

Schema digests pinned at proposal time. The registry's schemas change when an adapter updates. A proposal permitted under one contract must be verified under that same contract, or an update silently reinterprets a decision the owner already made. A result that violates the pinned schema is recorded failed and the drifted payload never lands.

Refusals are recorded, cancellation is bounded. A silent drop leaves the runner retrying for ever and the owner with no record of a decision they made. A runner may withdraw before a decision but never after execution begins — an effect that may already have happened cannot be un-asked.

PART, and the honest gap is the one this work keeps finding: NOTHING CALLS IT. There is no typed host operation to execute (3.5), no owner-facing permit surface, and no sweeper wired over listExecuting(). I am recording that plainly rather than marking DONE, because "built and unreachable" has been the defect in seven of the eleven items before this one. Verification: 13 tests, 7 mutations, all caught (one of my first mutations was a no-op — an appended comment rather than a behaviour change — and was redone). Tests: 6,385 core, 2,839 server, 3,293 mobile; typecheck and lint clean.

WS-3.5 — typed host operations (part)

One sentence is the whole rule: the runner supplies the PARAMS, Dina supplies the AUTHORITY. That is enforced structurally rather than by checking — the context an executor receives carries the validated params and the asking install's identity, and has no field for a credential, a session, or a key. A runner cannot smuggle authority through a payload because there is nowhere to put it. Everything an executor needs in order to ACT is closed over at composition time by the boot that owns those things.

A thrown executor settles outcome_unknown, never failed. A socket can die after the bytes have left, so a throw cannot distinguish "never happened" from "happened and the wire died". Calling it failed invites a retry that sends twice. The asymmetry is deliberate and costed: a false "unknown" costs an operator a manual check, a false "failed" costs a duplicate effect. Executors that CAN characterise a failure return failed themselves and stay retryable.

"Bounded" is a security property, not a performance note. An unbounded search lets a runner page an entire index through a channel the owner approved for one lookup. The cap is applied in Core after the search returns, and the request carries no limit field at all — so there is nothing for a runner to widen.

Mutation distinguished a layered pair from dead code, again. The dispatcher's `permitted` pre-check survived deletion, because the broker's own CAS catches the same case. Defeating BOTH lets an unpermitted proposal execute (11 tests red), so they are layered rather than duplicated: the pre-check reports which state blocked it and avoids parsing params for a proposal that cannot run, the CAS makes two concurrent dispatchers resolve to one winner. Kept and documented, where the redundant buyer check in WS-4.3 was deleted — the difference is whether removing both changes behaviour.

PART: only the bounded search exists. D2D send, publication candidate and connector broker are not written, and no boot registers an executor — so 3.4 and 3.5 are now reachable from each other but still not from a running node. WS-3.7 — the update-rebind coordinator (part)

A plugin update is three writes that must be one. Prior-CID authorizations, the install's new CID, and every listing pinned to the old CID. Each pair being out of step is an ordinary interleaving, not a crash, and each has a distinct cost: a CID that moves without its listings takes the supplier silently off the market (ingress refuses every query as binding_stale); listings that move without the CID fail the same way from the other side; a CID that moves without authorizations terminalizes in-flight tasks and stops a buyer's open order being answerable. So the unit of change is the transaction.

The claim is testable because the two tables share a database. service_configs and plugin_installs both live in Tier-0, so the listing rewrite is synchronous SQL inside the install's own transaction rather than a second write hoping to land. Tests drive real SQLite for exactly this reason — a pair of in-memory maps would let the atomicity claim be made without being earned.

Design decision: the in-memory listing cache adopts only after commit. service_config answers reads from a process-local map. Updating that map inside a transaction that can still roll back produces the same split-brain the transaction exists to prevent, pointing the other way. So the rewrite returns a committer and the coordinator runs it once the rows are durable. A new adoptServiceConfigInMemory exists for this and deliberately skips validation: the caller has already committed the row, and re-validating here could reject a listing the database has accepted, leaving memory and disk disagreeing with no way back.

Authorizations carry the PRIOR schemas, not the current ones. A drained task is judged against the contract it was created under. Reading the live manifest at claim time would let an update retroactively change what an in-flight task was allowed to return. The test fixture's new manifest renames both schema fields so this is visible rather than asserted. Two entries per prior capability, not one: drain covers tasks already claimed and expires; lifecycle_continuity admits NEW prior-major lifecycle calls and has no expiry, because §9.13 serves them until the orders are terminal and no clock knows when that is. It is released explicitly.

A mutation that survived was worth more than the six that died. Making the rebind publish to the cache inside the transaction changed nothing, because the only rollback any test exercised (a lost CAS) aborts before the rebind runs. The discipline was untested for the ordering where it actually matters. Adding "the transaction fails AFTER the rebind" killed it. Seven mutations verified in total; one earlier attempt was a no-op I caught by asserting the substitution count rather than trusting a green run.

Tradeoff: stores resolve per use rather than at construction. The install registry and the drain store are wired at different points of different boots — installs in storage/init.ts, drains in boot_service.ts on mobile. A coordinator that captured them would hold a null on whichever boot wires them later. Resolving per use and refusing stores_unavailable when either is missing removes the boot-order coupling entirely, and matches the decision already taken for the supplier DID.

Open, and stated plainly: nothing calls this yet. applyUpdate and setPendingUpdate had no production caller before this work — the owner-facing update discovery and consent flow has never been built, and that flow is the real owner of the boot wiring. I chose not to install a global singleton no boot reads, because a wired orphan is still an orphan. Also open: the Tier-0 runner is re-entrant, so calling apply from inside a larger transaction would let the cache adopt a rebind the outer transaction could still roll back — documented on the class, not defended in code. Verification: 11 tests, 7 mutations killed, typecheck and lint clean.

WS-3.8 — prior-major lifecycle continuity (part)

The spec is unusually direct here, and it is worth quoting the shape: a lifecycle request names a purchase order, Core reads that order's pinned major from the order-reference record and dispatches to that major's retained handler set, and "the current negotiation manifest is never consulted." So the routing key is the ORDER, not the listing. After an update the listing has already been rebound to the new CID (WS-3.7) — following it would answer a buyer under a contract their order was never opened against.

Deviation: I pin by the serving MANIFEST CID, not by the protocol major. The spec says major; the drain-authorization table is keyed (install_id, prior CID, capability); and a plugin manifest declares no protocol major anywhere, so there is nothing to derive a major from without inventing a new manifest field and a mapping that could disagree with the CID. A CID implies exactly one major, so pinning by CID is strictly finer-grained and uses the key both sides already share. `commerce_order_refs` gains `serving_manifest_cid`; "released per major once its last order is terminal" becomes "per prior CID". Flagging this as the one place I departed from the letter of §9.13.

Design decision: the serving CID is a CALLER-supplied admission input, not a node-wide setting. A node may run several supplier plugins. A single "current manifest" read at admission would stamp an order with whichever plugin happened to be asked about, and route its later lifecycle requests into another plugin's schemas — a correctness bug, not merely over-refusal. It is optional because a supplier need not be plugin-backed at all; absent, the order records '' and no prior-manifest routing applies, which is right, since nothing served it under a contract that could be superseded. Re-adopted orders (§16.2, rebuilt from a counterparty's held evidence) record '' deliberately: the evidence names the protocol version but not the manifest, and guessing the current one would claim a contract this node cannot vouch for.

Every authority field on a continuity envelope comes from the authorization. Params schema, result schema, approved scope hash, action class, effects idempotency, context bound — all recorded at rebind from the prior manifest. The claim guard performs the mirror check and re-derives the same fields from the same row, so the two sides cannot drift. A capability that has left the current manifest entirely is still served, which is the whole point.

Refusing is the honest failure. When no continuity lane was retained, ingress returns a typed lifecycle_continuity_unavailable rather than falling back to the current manifest. A fallback would parse the buyer's request against a contract their order was never opened under, and "answered wrongly" is worse than "not answered". A drain entry alone does not admit a NEW lifecycle request — drain covers work already claimed at the rebind moment; only continuity admits new arrivals.

A schema guard caught my fixture, again. Six tests failed with install_unavailable after an update that reported success. The cause: rowToInstall quarantines any row whose current_version column disagrees with its pinned manifest's own version, and my fixture bumped the column while reusing a manifest that still said 0.1.0. The guard was right and the fixture was wrong — the third hand-built plugin fixture this work has gotten wrong, which is itself the argument for how strict these mappers are.

Open: nothing yet calls releaseContinuity when the last prior-manifest order goes terminal — that needs a non-terminal count per CID, and the store today counts by protocol major (countReservedByMajor). And no submit_order wire path exists, so outside tests every order records an empty serving CID and the continuity branch is unreachable in production until that path is built. Closing the order round trip (§9.9, part)

A buyer must never receive the runner's JSON. An acknowledgement is a signed commercial record: it settles the quote hold, opens the status chain, and becomes the receipt both sides reconcile against later. Bridging the plugin's raw answer would hand the buyer a claim nothing signed, leave the hold unsettled, and leave no chain to ask about. So the runner DECIDES and Core RECORDS — transformInboundOrderResult intercepts a completed submit_order and replaces the result with what decideOrder produced.

Design decision: an unreadable answer leaves the order UNDECIDED. Every coercion available here — treating an unknown shape as acceptance, or as rejection — is a guess about whether a supplier meant to commit. The safe reading of "I cannot tell" is to record nothing and let the decision sweeper own it. The tests assert that no decision reaches the engine on any malformed answer, which is the part worth pinning: the refusal is only meaningful if it also declines to write.

Two failure directions, deliberately asymmetric. If the seam throws, the runner's own answer is sent — a completion that already landed must not vanish because the transform failed. If the seam runs but Core cannot record the decision, the runner's answer is likewise sent, and the order stays open. Neither case fabricates an acknowledgement.

The buyer is the authenticated sender, always. The purchase order id comes from the dispatched params but the buyer DID comes from the transport-authenticated envelope, so a runner naming someone else's buyer cannot redirect a decision onto an order it was never asked about. Mutation-verified by making it prefer the params field.

Tradeoff: the workflow layer gained a hook rather than an import. ingressResultTransformer is injected, so the workflow layer still knows nothing about commerce — it owns only the ordering, transform then send. It is wired once in workflow_plane.ts, which both boots go through, rather than at each boot: the option every root must remember is the option one of them forgets.

So all three §9.9 outcomes are now carried; the seam checks shape only, because a second opinion here could only disagree with the one that counts. WS-1.8 — the catalog publication wire contract (§10.2/§10.3, part)

The spec states one trust chain and the module is that chain: supplier repo proof → current snapshot pointer → immutable snapshot metadata → canonical payload root → bounded catalog pages. Every hop recomputes, which is what makes the feed host transport rather than authority — a modified page fails its digest, a swapped payload fails the root, a forged pointer fails the chain.

Design decision: catalog commitments are NOT §9.12 digest domains. §9.12 fixes ten record digests and the frozen conformance vectors pin every one; that list is a closed vocabulary for negotiation and lifecycle RECORDS. A page digest and a payload root are content commitments over published bytes — a different thing with a different lifetime. Adding them to the frozen list would have broken the vectors and quietly widened a deliberately closed set, so they get their own dina:commerce:catalog:v1: prefix and a test asserts the two domains cannot collide.

Tradeoff: an ordered commitment, not a Merkle tree. A tree would let a verifier check one page against the root without fetching the others — a real benefit, but only if inclusion proofs ship with it, and §10.2 bounds catalogs precisely so a consumer CAN fetch every page. Publishing a tree without proofs would advertise a guarantee the wire format does not carry. A tree stays additive if paging ever outgrows the bound.

Two mutations SURVIVED, and both found something real. Sorting the page digests before committing broke no test — the payload root's order-sensitivity was undefended, even though the docstring claimed order was committed. And removing page_index from the page digest broke nothing either, which exposed a wrong claim in my own comment: I had described the index binding as closing a reordering attack. It does not. Pages with different content already have different digests, so a reorder of those is caught by content alone; pages with identical content are interchangeable by definition, so swapping them changes nothing a consumer could observe. The binding is a correctness property — a page record cannot be served from a slot it does not claim — and the comment and test now say exactly that instead of overclaiming.

Update, same session: a PUBLISHER now exists (commerce/catalog_publisher.ts), so the contract has a producer. It runs the CONSUMER's own verifyCatalogPointerAdvance on its own output — one rule, checked by the same code on both sides, so a producer cannot drift from the verifier and a fork is refused before anything reaches the wire. Two further decisions the tests forced: an over-large catalog is REFUSED rather than truncated (a silently shortened full-state snapshot omits products with nothing in the record to say so), and WITHDRAWAL IS TERMINAL for a catalog_id — a tombstone names no snapshot, so nothing can link to it, and relaunch means a new id. That last one only surfaced because a test tried to publish after withdrawing and got a confusing digest-mismatch error; the rule is now explicit with its own message.

§10.3 feed hardening, built as PURE POLICY. A catalog feed URL is supplier-controlled input aimed at our own network stack — the classic SSRF shape, and the spec says to treat it as hostile. The module performs no I/O, deliberately: the dangerous part of a fetcher is the decision, not the socket, and a decision function can be tested against every address form an attacker would try. Covered: HTTPS-only as an ALLOW-list (not a deny-list — file:, gopher: and friends are all ways to read something local), credentials-in-URL refused, private/loopback/link-local/CGNAT/multicast literals blocked in v4 and v6 including the IPv4-mapped form, redirect targets re-validated exactly as the original, a content-type allow-list, and BOTH a decompression ratio and an absolute cap because neither implies the other. Anything it cannot parse is blocked rather than guessed at.

The limit of it, stated in the module itself: a URL check cannot stop DNS rebinding. A name that resolves publicly at validation can resolve to 169.254.169.254 at connect. So isBlockedAddress must ALSO run against the address actually connected to, on every hop, and a fetcher that only validates the URL has not implemented this policy. A mutation taught me something here too: killing the IPv4-mapped unwrap broke only one test, because a real URL parser normalises [::ffff:127.0.0.1] to the hex form and the conservative fallback catches that instead. Both forms are now pinned separately, since different code blocks each.

Update, same session: an INGESTER now consumes all of it (commerce/catalog_ingest.ts), so neither the policy nor the verifiers can be routed around. The design point: the injected transport is DUMB. A transport that took a URL and returned bytes would let a caller fetch first and ask questions after — and the interesting failure is exactly the request that should never have been made. So the ingester clears the URL before the first call, hands the transport something already approved, and requires it to report the address it ACTUALLY connected to so that can be re-checked on every hop. The pointer stays the authority: a snapshot that is internally perfect but is not the one the pointer names is refused, because substituting one publication for another is what the pointer exists to stop. Tests drive the real publisher's output through the ingester, so the two halves check each other rather than fixtures either invented. Open: the AppView side (Jetstream ingest, PostgreSQL projection, xRPC search) is unbuilt — this is the verification pipeline, not the index.

Deep review of the catalog work found two real gaps, both now fixed. CATALOG_FEED_LIMITS.maxMillis was declared and never read — a dead constant that made the module LOOK like it enforced a time cap. And there was no aggregate byte budget: a snapshot may legitimately name a thousand pages, so "each page is under 8MB" is not a limit on anything an attacker cares about — a compliant feed could cost gigabytes. Both are now one budget shared across the whole ingest, checked BEFORE each request so a spent budget stops the next call rather than paying for it first. A mutation confirms the budget is shared rather than per-fetch, which is the version of this bug that would look correct in review.

A third candidate I looked at and rejected: the snapshot rkey is the 64-char hex digest. That is a valid ATProto rkey (charset and length both fine) and it is content-derived as §10.2 requires, so there is nothing to fix — noting it because "looks unusual" is not the same as "is wrong", and the check was worth making.

WS-0.7 — rehydration through the ingress validator (part)

The asymmetry this closes. A record arriving on the wire was validated before anything acted on it. The SAME record read back from the receipt store was JSON.parse(…) as PurchaseOrderProposal — a promise to the compiler and a check of nothing. Five sites did this, and every one of them fed decision logic written on the assumption that ingress had already vetted the value.

Why it is worth closing even though the store is not an attacker. This is an integrity problem, not a security one, and the difference is in how it fails. A receipt written by an older build, truncated by a half-finished migration, or corrupted on disk flows straight into a commercial decision — and the first symptom is a wrong outcome rather than an error. Re-running the validator turns that into a loud refusal at the point of reading. It matters that the validator RE-DERIVES the digest: a test states this explicitly by showing a tampered order that is structurally perfect, carries the original digest field, and is still refused.

Design decision: the helper returns the failure rather than throwing. Each caller knows what an unreadable record means in its own context, and they genuinely differ — the decision path reports a store-integrity error, loadRetainedQuote treats it as "not retained" because its contract is "the quote a digest names, or null", and the recovery sweeper SKIPS that row so one corrupt receipt cannot stop every other timed-out order from being decided. A throw would have flattened three correct behaviours into one.

Update, same session — the ingress casts are gone too. Rather than assertion functions (asserts value is T), which read badly at call sites and cannot be composed, the wire package gained readPurchaseOrderProposal / readSignedQuote: they validate and TYPE in one step, returning the record or the reason it is not one. The existing validate… functions delegate to them, so there is ONE implementation and the two shapes cannot drift about what "valid" means. Every as PurchaseOrderProposal / as SignedQuote in src/commerce is now gone.

WS-2.5 + WS-2.6 + WS-1.6 — the fence path was a way around the deadline (§9.11, §16.2)

The restore fence is how a supplier that lost records rejoins a chain the buyer can still prove. It has a verifier on each side: the supplier signs one (signRestoreFence), the buyer accepts it (verifyRestoreFence). Reading them side by side, the two sides did not agree about anything.

The buyer enforced two rules the supplier did not. It checks the fenced fulfilment against the ORDER's accepted lines, and it checks the fence's state is legal from the predecessor. The supplier copied both forward verbatim and never loaded the order at all. That is not a harmless duplication gap: it means Core signs a record the buyer must reject, and the order strands — the precise outcome the fence exists to prevent. A restore is also the one moment the two halves can genuinely disagree, since the order reference and the status receipts are separate tables and can come back from different backup vintages.

The epoch bar had the same shape. Both sides require the fence to raise the epoch, but against different heads: ours locally, theirs at the buyer. After a restore the buyer's head sits ABOVE ours, on records we lost, and its epoch comes with it — so clearing our own bar says nothing about clearing theirs. The bar is now the higher of the local head and the highest epoch among the verified presented receipts, which for a conforming buyer is its head.

And NEITHER side applied the dispute deadline. verifyStatusSuccession refuses delivered → disputed once the window closes. verifyRestoreFence allowed the same move and never looked at the window, because it took no clock at all. A supplier could therefore dispute an order whose window shut months ago by marking the record restore_fence: true — a recovery mechanism used to escape a deadline, which is the shape of every privilege escalation. The verifier now takes the RECEIVER's at_iso, required rather than optional, because an omitted clock that skips the check reproduces exactly the bug. Recorded in the conformance changelog as breaking.

WS-5.3 — the publication leakage gate (§12.1, §23)

The last thing between a supplier's private data and a permanent public record. This gate is different in kind from every other validator in the pack: a rejected order can be resubmitted and a bad quote expires, but a catalog snapshot is published to a PDS, indexed by an AppView, and content-addressed — it does not un-publish. A credential that reaches one is disclosed permanently, to everyone. So a false negative costs unboundedly and a false positive costs a supplier renaming a column, and every judgement call below goes the refusing way.

Two independent rules, and neither subsumes the other. A closed public-field vocabulary stops internal_cost, supplier_notes and erp_connection by not KNOWING them — which is why it survives a field name nobody has thought of yet. A secret-shaped-value detector then covers the residual case the vocabulary cannot reach: a token pasted into description, which is a field a catalog is supposed to have. Rule 1 alone publishes a key in a legal field; rule 2 alone publishes a cost column that looks like nothing in particular.

The gate lives INSIDE `buildCatalogSnapshot`, before pagination and before any digest. WS-5.2 — supplier catalog import (§12.1, FR-S2, FR-S3)

A supplier's catalog already exists, in a spreadsheet. Any design that starts by asking them to re-enter it in a form gets a ten-row catalog for the demo and nothing afterwards — so the importer takes the CSV they have and tells them exactly what is wrong with it.

One rule shapes the whole module: a bad row is never dropped. The tempting behaviour — import what parses, skip the rest, report a count — is wrong in a way that is hard to see. A catalog snapshot is FULL STATE (§10.2): publishing it says "this is everything I offer". A supplier whose 40-row file imported 37 has published a catalog that silently omits three products, and nothing in the record says so. Buyers do not see an error; they see a supplier who does not stock the thing. So an import yields every row or none, and the findings name which rows and why. Unknown COLUMNS are reported for the same reason — a column silently ignored is a column the supplier believes they published.

Two judgement calls that look like fussiness and are not. Whitespace is trimmed but case is NOT folded: a trailing space is a spreadsheet artefact and refusing it would be hostile for no safety gain, while a vocabulary that folded case would have two spellings for one unit — and two spellings is how implementations come to disagree about how much was ordered. And defaultScheme is REQUIRED rather than defaulted, because guessing between gtin and sku picks the product's IDENTITY, and §9.4 forbids merging identity by name.

Variant parents resolve after every row is read, so a spreadsheet listing a variant above its parent imports cleanly. Refusing on row order would be an accident of how the supplier sorted their file, not a fact about their catalog.

The importer does NOT check for leakage, deliberately. buildCatalogSnapshot owns that gate and owns it alone, so there is one place a leak can be stopped and no second copy to drift from it. A test pins the division: a secret in a KNOWN column (`description`) passes import and is refused at publication. Two gates for one rule is two places to disagree.

Two initially crashed on unreachable-code type errors and were redone shape-preserving. 17 tests; one of my own cases was wrong (I expected a trailing space to be refused). WS-6.1 + 6.2 — hard filters, then a deterministic score (§13.2–§13.4, FR-B6, FR-B7)

How Sancho picks between the quotes he collected. Two stages, and the ORDER is the rule: hard filters remove offers that do not meet the stated requirements, and only what survives is scored. §13.2 is explicit about this and the reason is not tidiness — a weighted score can always be dragged across a hard requirement by making one factor extreme enough. A requirement that participates in a score is not a requirement. The test that pins it gives a FREE offer that cannot supply the quantity and checks it never appears at all.

No LLM in the arithmetic path (FR-B6). Integer basis points throughout, ties broken on quote id rather than arrival order, and the score recomputable from the components the buyer was shown. A model may write the requirements and read the explanation back; it never touches the comparison, because a ranking that cannot be recomputed is one nobody can dispute.

Every removal is reported. "Why isn't ChairMaker here" is the first question anyone asks of a shortlist, and a comparison that cannot answer it is one the buyer cannot trust. Currency is never converted to make an offer comparable — a rate applied here would be this node inventing a commercial term. An unreadable expiry reads as EXPIRED, not as "no expiry".

§13.4, the rule I care most about here: missing data is explained, never substituted. A new supplier has no PeerLens history. Scoring that absence as zero makes the trust system a barrier to entry; scoring it as full marks rewards having no history at all. So the factor is left out of THAT offer's score, the weight actually applied travels with the score, and the omission is named. Two tests hold both failure directions: a newcomer must not lose to a badly-rated incumbent on identical price and lead time, and must not beat an excellent one either.

Design decision, and I got it wrong first. Price and lead time are RELATIVE — "cheapest" is only meaningful against the other offers, so they normalise across the shortlist. Trust is ABSOLUTE: 4000 means the same thing whoever else happened to quote, and normalising it would stretch the least-bad supplier in a weak field up to full marks, telling the buyer that a mediocre record is excellent because the competition was worse. My own test expected the top offer to score 10000, which only holds if trust is normalised too. The consequence is worth knowing when reading a score: an offer winning every relative factor still cannot reach 10000 unless its rating already is.

Open question. The weights (price 60%, lead time 25%, trust 15%) are fixed in v1 rather than buyer-tunable. Tunable weights are a real feature and also the thing that would make two Dinas rank the same offers differently — so they wait until there is somewhere to record the chosen weights alongside the decision. Say if you would rather have them configurable now.

WS-5.9 — bounded quote fan-out (§12.3, §20.17)

A buyer asks "who can supply 500 oak chairs". The obvious implementation queries every supplier the index returns, which turns ONE local action into N outbound D2D messages to N independent nodes. At that point the buyer's Dina is a traffic amplifier: one tap produces load proportional to how many suppliers exist, and a peer repeating it in a loop has a distributed flood with someone else's name on the envelopes.

So the cap is a hard ceiling, not a default. A caller asking for more is CLAMPED rather than refused — refusing tempts a caller into a workaround, and the workaround will not have a cap at all. The amplification test runs the planner at 10, 100, 1,000 and 10,000 candidates and asserts the outbound count never moves.

One query per SUPPLIER, not per listing. Six listings from one manufacturer would otherwise take six of the eight slots, turning a cap meant to protect the network into one that narrows the buyer's comparison.

Trust orders but never excludes. A supplier nobody has rated yet sorts last and still gets asked if there is room. Excluding them at fan-out would make the trust system self-fulfilling — no queries, so no orders, so no reviews, so no trust, so no queries — and that is a barrier to entry dressed as a safety feature. Selection is deterministic down to the DID, so the buyer's fan-out is never a function of someone else's pagination.

A mutation showed one of my guards was doing nothing. Replacing Math.max(0, …) with a huge negative bound broke no test: a negative cap already selects nothing, because length >= cap is true from the start. Rather than delete the clamp or leave it looking load-bearing, I found what it actually protects — the REPORTED capApplied, which a caller puts in front of the owner when explaining a short shortlist, and which should not read -1. Now asserted, and the code says which half is behavioural and which is presentational.

WS-6.6 — competitor and probing resistance (§14.3, §20.10)

A competitor does not need to breach anything to hurt a supplier: they need only ask. A hundred quote requests at varying quantities draw the price curve, the volume breaks, and which products can actually be sourced. Every individual answer was legitimate — the harm is in the aggregate, which is why no single-request check can see it.

Two defences, answering different halves. A per- counterparty BUDGET over a window is the only thing that sees the aggregate. A UNIFORM refusal stops the refusal itself becoming the oracle — without it a prober stops reading the answers and starts reading the shape of the "no", learning the catalog before the budget even runs out. A budget with distinguishable refusals leaks early; uniform refusals with no budget leak to anyone patient. Neither is the other's fallback.

The wire refusal is a single constant, not a message built per case. Two refusals differing by one word are two a prober can tell apart, and every "helpful" detail — which product, whose budget, what window — is a bit of the catalog handed over. The real reason survives in a `reason` field that never crosses the wire, and a test greps the constant for the words that would leak.

Remaining budget is reported on SUCCESS only. A countdown on a refusal is a side channel: watch it fall and you have measured the policy, which tells you exactly how slowly to probe.

Two judgement calls. The unknown budget is small but not zero — a stranger has to be able to become a customer, and §20.10's concern is the curve rather than the first question. And the count is per counterparty, never global: a global counter lets one busy customer exhaust everyone's budget, which converts a privacy defence into an outage a competitor can trigger deliberately by being noisy.

Open question. The default budgets (60/hour known, 5/hour unknown) are a guess at where a real customer stops and a prober starts. I have no usage data; if you have a feel for how often a buyer legitimately re-quotes, that number is worth setting deliberately rather than by my estimate.

Six mutations, six killed: the refusal naming its reason; a global rather than per-peer counter; the window ignored; unknown peers given the known budget; blocked standing ignored; remaining budget leaked on a refusal. 12 tests. Verification: core 6,709; typecheck and lint clean.

WS-6.4 — hierarchical product evidence (§13.6, FR-B13, FR-B14)

"This chair has forty good reviews" and "chairs in this family have forty good reviews between them" are different claims. The first is about the exact variant a buyer is about to order; the second may be about a different finish, a different size, or a discontinued predecessor. Presenting the second as the first tells a buyer that a thing has a track record it does not have.

The failure is easy to reach because the convenient data structure is a single count. Sum forty inherited and two exact into "42 reviews" and the information that would have let a buyer discount it is gone, irreversibly, one line into the pipeline. So exact and inherited are never summed, never merged, and always labelled — they travel as separate groups all the way to the card, and there is deliberately NO function returning a bare number. That function would be called, its result rendered next to the product name, and the distinction lost at the last step.

FR-B14 runs both ways and both are tested: inherited must not be presented as exact, and exact must not be presented as inherited — understating what is known about the exact variant makes a buyer discount evidence they should have trusted. The headline returns NULL when nothing is known rather than a zero, because a zero beside a product name reads as a bad rating.

Writing this exposed two real defects in WS-5.2, both hidden by a cast. The importer built {scheme: 'sku', value} and cast it to ProductRef. `sku` is not one of the four §9.3 schemes (gtin | manufacturer_sku | dina_subject | custom), AND manufacturer_sku requires an issuer_did without which the reference is ambiguous across suppliers — two of them may both call something CHAIR-1. So every imported catalog carried product references the protocol's own validator rejects. Fixed by building and VALIDATING rather than asserting: the importer now takes the supplier DID, maps a CSV sku to a scoped manufacturer_sku, and runs validateProductRef. mpn is no longer an importable SCHEME — a manufacturer part number is issued by someone other than the supplier, so scoping it to the supplier's DID would assert an authority they do not have. It remains a descriptive column.

And the leakage gate then caught a third. With real product refs, publication started failing: issuer_did was not in the public-field vocabulary, so no scoped product reference could ever be published. It is part of the identity and public by construction; admitted along with variant_digest. Three defects in one chain, each found by the next layer refusing to accept what the previous one produced — which is the argument for making layers refuse rather than coerce.

Six mutations, six killed: pools merged; the headline dropping its scope; zero instead of null; unrelated evidence folded in; inherited standing in for absent exact; ancestor order reversed. One was a no-op first and was redone as the actual FR-B13 failure. 13 tests. Verification: core 6,722; typecheck and lint clean.

WS-6.3 — per-task context projection (§13.5, §11.3, FR-P3)

WS-2.8 — two fields, two rules, one write (§9.12, §16.2)

The receipt store is content-addressed, so the record body is self-proving. What it could not show is HOW this node came to hold the document — signed here, or received from a counterparty over an authenticated envelope. That is what a dispute turns on: “we agree this is the document” is rarely the argument, “you sent it to me, here is the envelope” is. The column has existed since CMC-1 and every caller wrote '{}'.

The write had to stop being one rule. `put` was ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING, which is RIGHT for the body: the digest addresses those bytes, so a second writer proposing different bytes under the same digest is a collision or an attack and the first writer must hold. It is wrong for evidence, and predictably so — the ordinary sequence is that a node records a document it built, then receives the same digest back from the counterparty carrying real authentication. First-writer-wins threw away the one a dispute needs. The two fields now have different rules under one write, matching what each field IS: the record is a fact about the document, the evidence is a growing list of facts about this node's encounters with it.

observedAt is deliberately absent from the dedup identity. A retried delivery of the same envelope arrives at a different millisecond and is the same fact; including the clock would turn the evidence list into a delivery log. The first sighting keeps its timestamp, because when it first arrived is the fact worth holding. Two deliveries under DIFFERENT keys both survive — “they sent it twice under two keys” is itself evidence.

Corrupt stored evidence reads as empty and is DROPPED rather than merged. Dropped because merging it would give anyone who could write that column a way to inject observations; read-as-empty rather than throwing because a receipt must not become unwritable when a metadata field goes bad — the record body is the thing under dispute, and losing access to it over metadata is the worse failure.

Five mutations, five killed. One was a finding of the familiar kind: the admission capture site had no test, so reverting it to '{}' left 589 commerce tests green. Also worth recording: the identity separator is written as the escape \u0000 rather than as a raw NUL byte. Three existing files (extension_broker.ts, matcher.ts, answer_cache.ts) use a literal NUL in source for the same purpose and are correct — but an invisible byte does not survive tooling reliably, and one of mine was silently altered during a mutation round-trip this session. The escape is the same value, visible. Verification: core 6,865 (+19), core-server 2,841; typecheck and lint clean.

WS-1.9 — two more frozen vector categories (§25.1)

Acting on the open question the scenario raised (§14.2)

I flagged this last round rather than fixing it, then reconsidered: the evidence was mine and the fix is narrow, so leaving it open would have been deferring a defect I had just paid for.

quote_unknown covers three different situations: an EXPIRED quote, one this node never held, and a retained request receipt it could not read back. Flattening them is correct on the wire — telling a stranger which one lets them probe the supplier's ledger — and wrong in a log, where an operator debugging a live refusal had exactly as much to go on as an attacker. That asymmetry is the whole point of §14.2 and it was being applied in only one direction.

The rejected outcome now carries an operator-facing detail alongside the acknowledgement. The first mistake is recoverable by contract; the second is a silent lost order. The sender's three outcomes carry their obligations in the TYPE rather than in a comment: not_sent is a promise that nothing crossed the boundary and is the only one that authorizes a resend, so a sender reporting a timeout as not_sent would authorize a duplicate.

The credential broker has no read API, and that shapes everything (§8.3, FR-P4, WS-9.3)

§8.3 says two things, and the second is the one that designs the module: connector secrets must go through a Core-owned broker, and a generic secret read API is forbidden. If a runner could ask “give me the ERP token”, every other control is decoration — the secret is then in a runner’s memory, its logs, and whatever it sends next. So a caller does not GET a credential. It asks the broker to PERFORM a named operation, and the broker is the only thing that ever sees the material.

Three consequences worth stating, because each was a choice:

  • The store is the one authority for the material AND the permission. An earlier shape took the grants as a constructor array; that made a revoked credential keep working until the process restarted, because the array was policy sampled at boot. Grants are read per call, so forget() stops the next one.
  • Credential-shaped params are refused, not stripped. A caller that put a token in params has a bug, and quietly removing it would leave that bug shipping secrets everywhere else it also passes them. §8.3 names “workflow payloads” explicitly.
  • §18.3 status is derived, never typed. lastResult comes from real brokered calls. A credential status somebody typed stopped being true the moment the other end rotated theirs, which is exactly when an owner needs to know.

Rotation is a replace, not a version, and it resets the verdict: keeping the old ok would tell an owner their new credential works before anything has used it. The rotation route answers {ok: true, resource} and nothing else — no prefix, no length, no hash, because each narrows a guess and none helps an owner decide anything.

A declaration is not evidence, and that is the whole of WS-9.4 (§15.5)

§15.5 ends: “No layer may declare idempotency merely because Dina deduplicates its own task row. The real external order system must deduplicate the effect; until a connector proves that with the same key and retention window, automatic resubmission stays disabled and ambiguity resolves through 12.7.”

So what the node accepts is an OBSERVATION, not a setting. The evidence route is deliberately not a switch: an owner records a probe — the same key sent twice — and the verdict follows from what the external system did. Five ways to fail it, each a real one:

  • No probe. The “merely because” case, and the default for every connector nobody tested.
  • A second order was created, or the two attempts resolved to different references. An EMPTY reference counts as a mismatch too: a connector that reports nothing has not shown the attempts converged, it has shown it cannot tell.
  • The two attempts were under a minute apart. An in-flight request lock satisfies that, and an in-flight lock is exactly the mechanism that stops working at the moment a retry matters — after a crash.
  • The window is shorter than §15.5’s sum (quote validity + reconciliation + commercial retention). An ERP that deduplicates for 24 hours is an honest NO to a retry after a two-day outage.
  • The observation is older than 90 days. A vendor can change their deduplication window in a release nobody told the supplier about; evidence with no expiry would let one probe authorise retries for the life of the install.

Open question. §15.5 names the sum but not the numbers, and the numbers decide whether any real connector can clear the bar. The default is 7 + 30 + 90 days = 127 days of key retention. That is demanding — many ERPs keep idempotency keys for 24 hours to 90 days — and §15.5’s own wording (“the CONFIGURED commercial-retention period”) says this is policy rather than protocol. The order of two writes is the safety property (§9.9 step 3, §15.5, WS-9.4)

mark effect_started → cross the boundary → record what came back, and never any other order. A crash between the mark and the call leaves a row saying “the effect may have fired”, which §9.9 refuses to time out — costly, and correct. The reverse would leave an executed external order behind a record that still looks safe to expire, which is how a supplier delivers goods against an order they believe they never accepted.

Two consequences fall out of it. An order that is ALREADY effect_started is never attempted again, even with proven evidence — crash recovery is not a retry of a known-failed attempt, and the honest answer there is reconciliation. And a broker refusal decided BEFORE any network I/O (wrong install, undeclared operation, no credential) is reported as refused_before_sending, because the broker settles every authorization check before it opens the secret store. operation_failed is deliberately absent from that set: a throw, a timeout or an error answer may still have created the order.

Every attempt carries the key the order was ADMITTED under, read from the record. A retry with a fresh key is not a retry; it is a second order wearing the same intention. The route ignores an idempotency_key in the request body entirely, and there is a test that passes one to prove it.

The fulfilment seam proposes; it never signs (§12.7, §9.11, WS-9.5)

The naive version of this seam reads the warehouse system’s status and signs it. That makes an external tool an authority over a chain buyers rely on: a mis-scan briefly reporting delivered would produce a SIGNED claim of delivery, and §9.11 has no way back from a signed state.

So reconcileFulfilment returns advance | unchanged | needs_attention and the caller signs. Three rules, each preventing something real: never backwards (a preparing webhook landing after a dispatched one would make a buyer watch their delivery un-happen); never a jump the §9.11 graph forbids; and an unchanged state is not an update (polling returns the same state constantly, and signing each one costs a signature apiece for a record that says nothing).

A refusal is needs_attention, not silence. A backwards or illegal report is evidence the two systems disagree, which is precisely the §12.7 case an operator has to see; dropping it would keep the disagreement invisible until a buyer complained.

Deviation worth stating. Fulfilment arrives by POLLING through the broker, not by webhook. It is slower, and it is the only version whose trust boundary I can state in a sentence.

§10.7 forbids an implementation by name, so the replacement had to be justified (§10.7, FR-A8, WS-10.3)

“Dina preserves the exact variant results and exposes material grouping disagreement instead of silently choosing whichever merge produces the highest score.” Taking the best answer is also the obvious implementation — ask three directories, keep the strongest, move on — and it is banned because the strongest answer is the one most likely to be wrong in the direction that costs money: a substitution authorised by whichever index was most enthusiastic.

So consensus is the floor, not the peak. An edge every consulted view reports carries the LOWEST confidence any of them gave it, which means a threshold is crossed only when all of them agree it is. An edge only SOME report is kept — §10.7 explicitly allows weaker semantic edges to improve recall — but capped below the standing bar, so it can be shown and can never inherit standing or authorise a substitution.

A contested edge authorises nothing whatever its number. That is stricter than the threshold alone, and deliberately: an edge two directories disagree about is exactly the edge that must not quietly change what a buyer receives. It is still SHOWN, with its dispute — hiding it would be the silent choice the spec forbids, made in the other direction.

Not every difference is material. Two views at 3100 and 3300 basis points agree about everything a caller can do with the edge, and reporting that would train an owner to ignore the display. A disagreement is material when it CHANGES AN ANSWER: the views land on different sides of a threshold, or they name different objects for a relationship that has one.

Duplicated thresholds, on purpose. An AppView deploys standalone and cannot import from @dina/core, and Core must not trust an index to say where the substitution bar is — a compromised one could lower it by reporting its own number. The three constants are therefore copied, and a test reads the AppView source as TEXT and compares. (Importing it fails: the AppView is ESM with .js specifiers Core's resolver cannot follow, and a guard that fails for a resolver reason is a guard somebody deletes for being flaky.)

A bug the mutation round found, in production rather than in a test (WS-10.3)

That is the worst case to miss — a directory that lists both candidates is the one that NOTICED the ambiguity. §10.7 cares about the disagreement, not about who is holding it. Promotion is a gate, and the additive rule is the half that bites (§11.1, §11.3, WS-10.6)

§11.1 is the argument for taking §11.3 literally: the existing official price_check uses a FLOATING-POINT price and permissive additional properties, and order_status has no pinned schemas at all. Both were promoted at some point by somebody who felt the semantics were stable, and commerce v1 consequently cannot reuse either.

So the seven proofs are checkable rather than asserted. “Stable semantics across multiple independent providers” is a count of distinct provider DIDs (not rows — one provider cannot be two by appearing twice) answering ONE pinned schema hash, at least twenty times each, over at least thirty days. “Interoperable failure codes” means every provider expressed both refused and unavailable: “you may not” and “not right now” lead a buyer to different next steps, and a provider that only ever emits one has not shown it can distinguish them.

The additive rule is checked FIRST, before any of that, because a promotion that would reinterpret an existing capability is refused however good its evidence is. What a promotion may do is ADD an official id and record the custom one as an alias; the custom id keeps working, because retiring it would break every install already calling it. The read side matters too: resolveCapabilityId reports via_alias, so a caller can SEE its id was translated rather than have the substitution hidden from it.

One survivor here was worth fixing: when providers disagree about the schema, the “stable” hash is the empty string rather than one of the competitors. Returning a competitor would hide the additive finding behind schema_not_stable, and an operator would fix the providers, re-promote, and only then discover the promotion also reinterprets. Both findings now surface together.

What the refactor is NOT (WS-0.2b / WS-0.3b)

It is not a unit of work, an identity map, or a place to accumulate changes — those would be a layer for its own sake over code that does not need one. The coordinator runs a function inside a transaction and knows whether one is open. The domain classes kept every line of their logic; what they lost is the decision about where a transaction starts, which is an application concern and was never theirs.

The service method names are the engine's OLD ones, deliberately. Every caller — routes, the ingress bridge, the decision path, six test suites — already speaks that vocabulary, and renaming them would have made a transaction refactor look like a behaviour change in the diff. Three of §3.4's four typed host operations were never written (§3.4, FR-P9 — WS-3.5)

A node signing into a wall looks exactly like a node nobody is buying from (§16.2 — WS-2.4)

§16.2 has one sentence that had no implementation: “Signing nodes also re-verify the live epoch on a bounded interval, so a forgotten pre-restore node converges”. The scenario is concrete. A supplier node is running. Somebody restores the same business identity on another machine, which publishes epoch 2 and voids the backup's quote capacity. The first node knows nothing: it cached epoch 1 at boot and goes on signing quotes and status updates at epoch 1 for ever. Counterparties reject those records — that is the hard enforcement and it lives elsewhere — but from inside, a node signing into a wall is indistinguishable from a node nobody is buying from.

Converging means stopping, not adopting. The obvious reading of “converges” is that the stale node picks up the higher epoch. That would be wrong twice over. It would resume signing beside the node that actually restored — two generations under one business identity, both “current” — while still holding the pre-restore capacity counters the fence had already voided on the other. And if observing a higher epoch made a node increment, two live nodes would supersede each other for ever. So supersession stops signing and waits for an operator; the legitimate route to a higher epoch stays establishAfterRestore(), which publishes its own increment and voids capacity in the same commit.

The refusal is what makes it enforcement. A revalidation whose only effect is a log line changes nothing about what the node signs. So the cached epoch carries a validated-at time and currentEpoch() refuses once it has gone unverified past a bound — which is the one part of this that is a judgement call, and it is recorded as an open question below.

Five distinct stop reasons, each a different repo fault: superseded; rolled back below what this node signed at; the record deleted (absence is not consent — this node published or adopted an epoch, so an empty repo means somebody removed the fence); the record replaced in place at the same number (the digest covers activated_at, so that cannot happen by accident); and a record that turns out to belong to another business. An unreachable repo is deliberately NOT one of them on a single failed read — a blip must not take a healthy supplier off the market — but the staleness bound turns a long run of them into a refusal, because “I cannot check” for long enough IS the forgotten node.

The phone had no epoch service at all. Asking what the other boot does found it again: getCommerceEpochService() was READ in two places on mobile and SET in none, so currentEpoch() threw on every commerce operation there. Invisible, for the same reason as every gap in this class — a node refusing everything is what a correctly fail-closed node also looks like. The repo protocol (CAS on swapRecord, an unreadable record refusing rather than reading as absent, the restore-fence decision) moved from the server app into @dina/home-node, and the server keeps only the genuinely server-shaped part: where the credentials come from.

A comment that described the intent while the wiring did something else. The §9.9 admission-recovery sweep was started inside the shared workflow plane, under a comment saying both boots got it from one place. They did not: the phone composes its own background work and never calls that plane. So on the product's primary surface no abandoned reservation ever timed out, every abandoned order held its quote capacity indefinitely, and the buyer was answered received_processing for ever. Both commerce ticks now start through one startCommerceSweepers per composition root, and a boundary guard fails if either root stops starting or stopping them — stopping matters as much, because a phone that kept the epoch tick running after an identity switch would go on re-reading the previous identity's repo.

The orphan ledger had a hole, and I found it while assuming the opposite. I read the admission sweeper as unwired, checked the ledger, and it was not listed — which looked like the guard failing. It was not: the sweeper was wired, in a package my grep had not covered. But the check I ran to prove it exposed a genuine weakness. The ledger counts a symbol as internally consumed when its own file names it more than once, and a class that names ITSELF in an error message (throw new Error(`CommerceAdmissionSweeper: intervalMs must be > 0`)) meets that bar with no call at all. Any such class could sit unwired and off the ledger. String literals are now stripped from the same-file count, with template interpolations kept — an interpolated symbol IS a reference, a message is not.

Three mutations were killed by the compiler rather than by a test, and that is a real result rather than a gap: the state machine is a discriminated union, so removing the unestablished or stopped guard makes validatedAtMs unreachable and the file stops building. The other nineteen were killed by tests.

The spec forbade the design I was about to build (§3.4 — WS-3.4)

I had the route mapped out — POST /v1/plugins/host-operations, plugin callers only, install resolved from the authenticated device — before reading §3.4's own sentence on the subject: “a runner requests an operation only by completing its current claim with a typed proposal ... the request rides the existing plugin task envelope and claim-token discipline — there is no separate in-process callback surface”. The route would have been exactly that surface: a second door into the same authority with none of the claim-token, idempotency, cancellation or lease discipline the task lane already enforces, and a runner holding one could ask for effects outside any claim it held.

So the lane is a completion interceptor. The runner completes its claim with a typed proposal; Core recognises it, brokers it, and the verified result reaches the runner as its NEXT task. Four rules follow and each is enforced rather than documented: the runner names an operation and nothing identity-shaped (a params install_id is REFUSED, not ignored); the capability comes from the pinned manifest AND the consent record, because a manifest capability with no consented scope hash is one the owner declined or has not yet seen; a PAUSED install cannot act on a claim it held while active, which is the window §14's pause creates; and a retry of the same idempotency key is never re-decided, so a runner that lost a response cannot spend a second permit for one asked-for effect.

The permit decision is the existing action plane, and that was also a spec instruction rather than my preference. §3.4: an effectful operation routes through the canonical plane “exactly like any other plugin effect: authority-domain evaluation, approval or standing-grant check ... and the deny-by-default safety floor”. So the decision runs evaluatePluginIntent over the operation's action_class — the same table, floors and first-N rule that govern every other plugin effect. A second policy here would have given the owner two consent surfaces that disagree, which is worse than either alone.

Two of my tests were wrong and the gatekeeper was right both times, which is worth recording because both were my misunderstanding of a rule I then went and checked. First: I set a standing approval in the test defaults and expected a card — but §3.4 says “approval OR standing-grant”, so a standing grant past the first N is supposed to run silent. Second: I expected a read to run silent and it carded, because §8 rule 5 says a CUSTOM capability id never floors below MODERATE (“the declared class is a consent label, not proof”). The fixed tests assert both halves of each rule instead of one, which is more than the originals would have caught.

A real defect fell out of building the sweep. settle read the proposal's state and then wrote without a compare-and-swap. Two settlers racing — the dispatcher finally hearing back, and the recovery sweep giving up on the same row — both passed the read and both wrote, last one winning. The loser could be the one holding the TRUE outcome: a swept outcome_unknown overwriting a verified completed turns a fact into a question, and an operator then chases an effect that was fine. finish now CASes on executing.

The sweep settles and never retries, and the deadline costs something I should name. A row in executing means the effect may have crossed the wire, so re-running it is how one purchase order becomes two; the sweep's whole job is to convert an open question into the terminal state that says so. But a host operation that is slow rather than dead gets swept, and its real result is then refused by the new CAS — the truth is lost and the owner sees an unknown for an effect that was fine. I picked that over the alternatives deliberately: no deadline leaves crashed rows open for ever, and a mutable terminal state would let a late settler overwrite a decision the owner has already acted on. Fifteen minutes is more than an order of magnitude beyond any operation this node ships, so a sweep of a live operation means something was already wrong.

The seam is now wired and the chain runs end to end. WorkflowService.complete() notices a typed proposal, DETACHED like the response bridge: the completion has already been CAS-confirmed, brokering is async, and a caller of complete() must never block on a network effect. A failure anywhere in the handler is reported, never thrown back — it cannot unwind a completion that is already durable.

The verified result reaches the runner as its NEXT claim on its own lane, in a new host_operation envelope block. That block is deliberately not params: the consented params_schema governs params, and an operation's result is not that shape, so putting it there would either fail validation or force the schema wide enough to accept both. It carries the terminal STATE verbatim, because a runner that saw only a result could not tell a refusal from a failure from an outcome nobody knows, and those three lead it to opposite next steps. A refusal is delivered too — a runner left waiting on a task that will never come is worse than a no. settleOwnerDecision gives the owner-approved route the SAME follow-up path as the auto-permitted one, so a runner cannot tell whether a human was involved.

Wiring the phone found the same divergence a third time. Mobile's WorkflowService never had ingressResultTransformer either — the §9.9 seam that answers a buyer with the acknowledgement Core signed instead of the runner's own JSON. The server plane has passed it since WS-3.8; the phone composes its own service and did not. So a supplier running on a phone was returning a runner's answer to a buyer. Both are now passed at both roots, through one factory.

One mutation survives and stays. The lane refuses on !decision.allowed || decision.mode === 'blocked', and every path in evaluatePluginIntent that sets mode: 'blocked' also sets allowed: false — so no test can reach the second clause and deleting it changes nothing today. It stays, documented in place, because the state it guards is the one where a permit would be worst: a decision whose two fields disagree must refuse rather than pick the permissive reading. Saying that plainly is more honest than contriving a test that fakes the contradiction.

The survivor hunt also paid for itself twice. It found a REAL gap — nothing completed a NON-plugin task through a service with a handler installed, so the envelope check was untested on the overwhelmingly common path — and a fixture bug: a capability without action_class builds an envelope the parser rejects, which is the parser working exactly as designed. The first version of the suite silently produced no follow-up because of it, and read as a broken seam.

Revocation, and the difference between two teardown APIs (§16.3, §16.4 — WS-11.3)

§25.6's manual journey ends "revoke and prove no further work", and the in-process half of that is now on the retailer↔manufacturer lane. The interesting part was not writing the scenarios; it was that my first version reached under the real door.

`markRevoked` is a PENDING-only tombstone. Used on an ACTIVE install it silently changes nothing, so my first three scenarios reported a revoked pack that carried on trading — and would have passed as soon as I "fixed" them to match. `remove` is closer but still only ONE step of the teardown. The real `uninstall` is the operation with the rules in it, and driving it turned three weak assertions into five that say something:

  • the uninstall is refused while an order is open, because every lifecycle capability a buyer needs — status, cancel, reconcile — answers through that install's binding, so tearing it down leaves the records intact and unreachable;
  • it is still refused after delivery. That is a recorded design decision now asserted rather than trusted: delivered is deliberately not terminal, because a buyer inside the dispute window can still dispute, and those are exactly the orders an uninstall would damage most;
  • once nothing is open it succeeds and fences the runner by revoking its paired device — which is what stops it acting, not the row's absence. A teardown that deleted the row and left the device paired would leave a runner that still authenticates;
  • a new order afterwards is refused install_unavailable, not no_plugin_binding: the listing still NAMES the install, so the honest report is that the named install is gone rather than that the listing was never bound. The two send an operator to different places;
  • a paused install HOLDS its queued order instead of losing it. §16.3's pause is a hold the owner can lift, and reading it as a teardown would drop orders they meant to resume.

Both times my expectation and the code disagreed, the code was right. That is the third time this session, and the pattern is worth naming: a scenario test written against what I assumed the API did is a scenario test that certifies my assumption.

The registry was empty, so the whole §3.4 lane was closed from the declaring side (§25.2 — WS-11.2)

WS-3.5 added three typed host operations and registered their EXECUTORS at both boots. WS-3.4 built the proposal broker and the completion seam. What nobody had done was register the operations in the ExtensionOperationRegistry — which both boots created EMPTY. The two halves answer different questions: the dispatcher says what the node can DO, the registry says what a capability may DECLARE and pins the schemas a proposal is checked against. With an empty registry every operation was refused operation_unregistered before reaching an executor, so the executors were unreachable and the lane was open on one side and closed on the other. Invisible for the usual reason: a node that refuses every brokered operation looks exactly like a node nobody asked.

The action classes are the policy, and they are the fail-closed reading. publication_candidate is read, because it validates and does not publish — classing it write would card an operation that changes nothing. d2d_send and connector_broker are write: the first puts bytes on the wire under this node's identity and cannot be taken back, and the second can submit a purchase order to an ERP under the same name. Classing an operation by the weakest thing it might do is how the strongest gets through silently.

Two more findings fell out. A PROVIDER-only capability cannot ride the tool lane — a hand-built tool task naming one is terminalized stale_authority rather than served, because the consented KIND is authority and the two lanes carry different context. An answer that was true and useless (§12.7, §16.2 — WS-2.3)

A supplier that lost an order re-adopts it from the buyer's held, supplier-signed acknowledgement rather than answering never_received — because answering "never received" against its own signature would invite a duplicate order. It then reported received_accepted, which is TRUE and useless: it hands the buyer back the document they just presented, and it tells a party who is about to wait for status updates that the order is live. It is not. Re-adoption rebuilds a REFERENCE, not the order's lines, its quote context, or its external state, and chain creation stays barred until the owner runs the §16.2 ceremony.

It now answers received_unresolved — §12.7's own phrase for "this supplier holds the order but cannot report where it is" — with the longer retry that suits something needing a human. Deliberately NOT a new outcome: the set is frozen with conformance vectors, and a fourth value would be a wire-major change to say what an existing one already says. The answer is the same on the poll that performs the re-adoption and on every poll after it, and it CLEARS: once the ceremony makes the order describable again, the same question returns received_accepted. The scenario test now walks that whole arc.

The scenario had been contradicting itself in writing. Two assertions and a comment, all in view of each other, disagreeing. The open item said "just needs a tick". It does not. (§12.7 — WS-7.7)

§12.7's buyer-side re-poll — the loop that asks a supplier again about an order whose outcome is unknown — is built, mutation-tested and unreachable. Both the WBS row and the orphan ledger recorded the reason as "no scheduler tick calls the sweep yet", which reads like ten minutes of wiring. It is not, and I only found that out by trying to do it.

sweepReconcilePolls takes ask: (…) => Promise<OrderReconcileResult | null>: a request that resolves to its own answer. The requester lane does not work that way. Every outbound service.query in this system is fire-and-forget, the answer arrives later as a correlated service.response, and there is no await-capable requester helper anywhere in Core — I checked for one before concluding it. Hanging a tick on this signature would mean inventing a correlate-and-await layer for ONE caller, sitting beside the durable correlation everything else already uses. That is the shape of a workaround.

The real work is a contract change, and it is worth stating precisely so the next pass does not repeat my hour. Split the sweep in two: ASK fires the order_reconcile query, advances nextPollAtMs and records that it asked; APPLY runs applyReconcileResult from the inbound response, exactly as the buyer's other lanes already do. The state machine and its rules — only never_received authorises resubmission, and only when no supplier-signed evidence was presented — are unchanged and already correct. It is the transport shape that is wrong, and I have corrected the record in all three places it was stated wrongly rather than leaving a note that makes the job look smaller than it is.

The re-poll, wired — and the second contract change nobody predicted (§12.7 — WS-7.7)

The note above was right about the split and wrong about it being the whole job. askReconcilePolls now fires the order_reconcile query and advances the clock; ReconcilePollSweeper runs it on a tick both composition roots start. That part went as described.

The sweep needs to REBUILD the question, and the buyer's record could not describe its own order. poll_count is a column precisely because §12.7 requires the loop to survive a restart — and after a restart the buyer knew it should ask and could not say what about. So order_digest, idempotency_key, protocol_version and service_rkey now live on commerce_buyer_orders beside the counter, written when the order is sent. Durability of the counter without durability of the question was durability in name only.

The rkey is not bookkeeping.   • One seam for two capabilities. Publish order is a failure model, not a data model (§10.2 — WS-5.1)

C-09 — every buyer-side write was load, await, then clobber

The buyer order store had no swap value, and every write to it is load → AWAIT → write: the executor sends, the poller asks a supplier. Without a revision the last writer wins, and the last writer is the SLOWEST one. Three consequences, all of them the expensive kind:

  • Two orders on the wire. The duplicate check at step 1 is a READ; two concurrent submissions both pass it. Only the insert is atomic — and the executor ignored its answer and sent anyway. A lost create now refuses.
  • A settled order un-settled. A send completing after a re-poll already recorded an acknowledgement wrote its own outcome_unknown over it, putting the order back into the state that invites a duplicate.
  • One authorization spent twice. "One authorization, one resend" is only true if the consume is atomic; two workers that both read the flag each sent.

The resolution is one function, settleBuyerOrder, because four call sites deciding what a lost race means would eventually disagree. A lost race is NOT "retry with my value" — my value is older than what is already there. If the live row is terminal, leave it; if it is not, re-apply the answer on top of the live row so the newer poll count survives and the answer is not dropped. One retry, never a loop: a second conflict means a third writer, and spinning on an order lane is worse than losing the write.

The terminal-wins branch first looked unkillable, and the reason is worth recording: the state machine ALSO refuses to move a terminal record, so preserving the state proves nothing about the resolver. What the guard actually saves is the WRITE — a fall-through re-applies and rewrites identical values at a new revision, on a settled order. The test now asserts the revision is unchanged, and the mutation dies.

C-03 — the reconcile gate demanded a field the protocol has never had

C-18 — continuity authority was never released, and the counter was the wrong one

Two defects, and the second only becomes dangerous once the first is fixed. releaseContinuity was written, tested, and called by NOTHING; continuity authorizations carry expiresAt: null on purpose, because no clock knows when an order ends. The counter it would have released on — countReservedByServingManifest — answers "is anything waiting to be DECIDED". Wiring the release to that counter would have revoked authority the buyer still needs, which is a worse failure than the one being fixed. The counter came first, and unfinished is now defined by the STATUS CHAIN.

Delivered still counts as unfinished. It is terminal only after its dispute window, which SQL cannot evaluate — so the query errs toward "still working". The cost is a prior CID kept alive slightly too long; the cost of the other direction is a buyer locked out of an order inside their window.

The release is a SWEEP rather than an event, because the moment a lane becomes releasable is the moment its last order goes terminal — inside the lifecycle engine, a commerce concern that must not reach into the plugin registry to revoke authority. X-11 — §12.7 re-adoption is inert in production, and its two halves disagree

The most consequential finding of this session, and the one I most want you to read. §12.7/§16.2's held-evidence re-adoption does not run at all on a real node, and it fails in a way where each half looks safe alone.

  • The buyer's installHeldEvidenceReader is called only in tests. Neither composition root installs one, so a real buyer presents NO evidence, ever.
  • The supplier's verifyHeldEvidence is also unwired at both boots. It fails closed, so re-adoption is refused even when evidence does arrive.

Individually each reads as cautious. Together: a supplier that lost state answers never_received; the buyer, having presented nothing, treats that answer as LEGAL; and never_received is the one answer that authorises a resubmission. That is the duplicate order the whole of §12.7 exists to prevent, reachable on any node today.

Why it cannot simply be wired up. Chasing it turned up a contract disagreement — the fifth comment/code divergence this session and the load-bearing one:

  • commerce-protocol/reconcile.ts: "signature is hex-encoded Ed25519 over the record's canonical JSON."
  • core/lifecycle_engine.ts's verifier: "Ed25519 over the retained D2D envelope."

One field, two contracts. A buyer cannot satisfy both, and because NEITHER side is wired, nothing has ever exercised the disagreement. This is why the gap survived every green suite: the tests install a reader that returns whatever the test wants, so the question of what a real signature IS never arose.

Design decision (reversible; tell me if you disagree). I am taking the ENVELOPE reading, for three reasons that are facts about the code rather than preferences:

  1. A supplier emits no per-record signature. OrderAcknowledgement has no signature field; authenticity comes from the signed D2D envelope that carried it. So the canonical-JSON reading asks the buyer to retain something the supplier never sends, and would need a wire-format change and a protocol major bump.
  2. The evidence vocabulary already models the envelope: receiptEvidence.receivedFrom takes {fromDid, observedAt, envelopeId, keyId, signature}. It was designed for exactly this and the buyer simply never called it.
  3. The verifier that will actually judge the evidence — Core's — already says envelope.

The signature is available where it is needed: receive_pipeline.ts verifies signatureHex before the buyer seam and just does not pass it on.

Built. And the envelope reading needed one correction to be true.

Building it exposed a hole in my own decision. An envelope signature is checkable only against the envelope's bytes, and I had planned to retain {envelopeId, signature} — an id and a signature, with the signed bytes thrown away. No supplier could ever have verified that. It would have replaced one unverifiable pair with another and looked wired the whole time.

So the buyer retains the message. HeldEvidence now carries a RetainedEnvelope — the six fields the D2D signature covers, in the shape the wire builder serialises: id, type, from, to (array), created_time (number), body (plaintext, pre-base64). The types are load-bearing: storing the timestamp as a string emits "created_time":"177…" where the signature covers "created_time":177…, and every verification fails for a reason no error message would name.

The pairing is the new attack, so Core binds it. {record, envelope, signature} invites presenting one message's genuine signature beside another message's record. verifyHeldRecord checks, in compiled code: the message came FROM this supplier; it went TO the buyer now presenting it; and the record's content digest appears among the signed body's own string values (walked over the parsed JSON, not substring-matched, so a digest inside an unrelated blob is not a match). The record's own validator recomputes that digest from its content, so digest-in-signed-body means the supplier put its name to a message carrying exactly this record. Only the cryptography is delegated to the injected callback — a boot that implements it loosely cannot drop the binding.

Both halves are built in Core (makeHeldEvidenceReader, makeHeldEvidenceVerifier) and installed at both composition roots. The reader being test-only for its whole existence is the argument: a defence each root must remember to install is one a root eventually forgets, so there is now one implementation to be right or wrong, phone and server alike.

Answer shape, worth knowing. Unverifiable evidence is refused non-disclosingly — not answered never_received. That is stronger than it first reads: never_received is the one outcome authorising a resubmission, so once evidence is presented the only safe answers are "verified, here is the decision" and "I cannot tell". "It never happened" is off the table.

What proves it. Seven unit cases drive the real verifier over a real keypair (wrong key, genuine signature over other bytes, tampered body, unresolvable key, malformed signature, and the honest control). The two-node journey — ChairMaker restores a backup from before Sancho's order, Sancho presents what he kept, ChairMaker re-adopts — now runs the real verifier rather than () => true. Its counterpart drives three forgeries (wrong key, addressed to another buyer, signed body that does not carry the record) and asserts none of them re-adopt. The commerce boundary test caught the stale "not yet wired" annotation on its own.

Open question, narrowed. The envelope reading is now implemented, so this is a question about what to do next rather than a blocker. Per-record supplier signatures remain the more robust design — evidence would survive outside the transport that carried it, and a buyer would not need to store message bodies — at the cost of a protocol major, a signing key threaded into the commerce runtime (it has none today), and a signature field on three record types plus their digest-exclusion rules. Say the word and I will do it; nothing built here forecloses it.

X-06/X-07 — a drained task skipped the one consent check that mattered

The update/rebind coordinator turned out to be built and wired at both boots: it snapshots the prior capability's schemas, creates the bounded drain and the unexpiring lifecycle_continuity entries, rebinds listings, and releases continuity only when the relevant orders are terminal. What was wrong was on the claim side, and it was an authority hole rather than a gap.

The shape of it. The claim guard deliberately skips its whole consent block for a drained task, and that is correct: after an update the capability may have left the current manifest entirely, so the drain entry is the consent proof. But one check inside that block was not bookkeeping — §11.2a's rule that an ingress task may dispatch only a capability consented as provider, and every other plugin task requires tool. Skipping the block skipped that too, and the drain row recorded no kinds to check against. So a continuity lane opened for a provider capability admitted a tool envelope on the same capability id, and the reverse.

The fix is to make the row able to answer. DrainAuthorization now carries authorizedKinds, taken from the PRIOR capability for the same reason the schemas are — the current manifest cannot vouch for what was approved under the old CID. The guard computes the required kind once and applies it on both lanes.

An empty list is a refusal, not a wildcard. Rows written before the column existed default to [], which reads as "this row cannot say which kinds were consented". Cannot-say has to refuse: the alternative is admitting an envelope onto a consent no row can show covered it. Same posture as the held-evidence verifier, and for the same reason.

Tests. Three cases — provider lane refuses a tool envelope, tool lane refuses a provider envelope (with a matching-lane control so the refusals are the check working rather than the lane being shut), and a no-kinds row refuses. Mutation-checked: stubbing the predicate to false fails exactly those three and nothing else. The type change also flushed out every hand-seeded drain row in the suite, which is the point — each now has to state which kind it authorized.

Still open on this row. §9.13 also asks that a continuity claim be bound to a specific non-terminal order at the authorized prior major. The prior major is already on the row (priorVersion) and the envelope carries prior_version, so that check is cheap; the order binding needs the envelope to name the order it continues, which today it does only inside params. I have not done either yet and am not counting them as done.

WS-8.1/8.2/8.3/8.5 — built, as @dina/managed-runtime

I raised the concern that a hosting product does not belong in the commerce WBS; the owner reaffirmed "complete all pending items". So it is built — in its own package, which satisfies both the instruction and §17's opening line. Nothing in it imports a vertical, and nothing in it knows what a quote or an order is.

The design is one rule, made structural. §17.2 permits sharing the control plane, AppView, MsgBox, Brain workers, the package cache, supervision, metering and backup — then constrains all of it: shared workers "never receive a reusable tenant master key or unscoped database handle". Every decision below follows from refusing to let that be a convention:

  • TenantRecord has NOWHERE to put a key. Not a nulled field, not an optional — no field. A design where the key could be stored and merely should not be is a design where one day it is.
  • ManagedBlobStore has no decrypt, not even an injected one. A hook the vendor could populate is a key the vendor holds. A tenant's export is decrypted by the tenant with the recovery phrase that already opens its vault, which is what makes "tenant-owned" a fact rather than a promise.
  • A CellLease names ONE tenant and one purpose and expires. There is no all-tenants lease and no way to derive a second tenant's from a first. LeasePurpose is a closed set, because a free-text reason would let a caller invent a purpose no cell knows how to refuse.

Cells register SEALED (§17.3), and lease REFUSES a sealed cell rather than waking it. Waking means key material becomes resident, so it is the tenant's own act — modelled as a separate method precisely so the two cannot be confused at a call site. A worker that could wake a cell by asking for work would make idle sealing decorative.

Judgement calls worth your eye:

  • A zero quota means "do not serve", not "unlimited". The fail-closed reading of an unset or suspended plan, and the classic way a suspended tenant keeps being served.
  • Fleet suspension is one call, not a loop over bindings. When a hosted runner is compromised the operator needs a stop that cannot half-apply; a loop failing partway leaves a subset of businesses served by a runner already judged hostile.
  • Refusals are ordered least-to-most specific. unknown_runner says nothing about which tenants exist; not_bound_to_tenant nothing about which installs do. A probe learns the least that is still true.
  • The blob store copies on write and re-digests on read. A caller reusing its buffer would otherwise rewrite stored history, and the digest check would then report corruption for a store that did nothing wrong. A blob failing its digest is REFUSED, never returned with a warning — it is exactly the object a tenant would restore from without looking.
  • Idle sealing is a sweep, not a timer. The caller owns the cadence, so a deployment schedules it and a test runs it at an instant of its choosing.

How this relates to Core's claim guard, which stays the last word. HostedRunnerRegistry.admits returning true is permission to TRY, never to claim — Core's guard then decides. It adds only the binding a single Core cannot see: which tenants a vendor identity is admitted to at all, and whether the fleet is suspended. Both are control-plane facts. Nothing here can make a claim succeed that Core would refuse, which is what keeps a compromised control plane from becoming a compromised tenant.

24 tests, all four isolation mutations killed (lease tenant check, zero-quota reading, vendor-to-any-tenant admission, store copy-on-write). Gate: 18,928 across nine packages, typecheck 0. One mobile flake (recovery-phrase idle timer) appeared and passed on re-run and in isolation twice — the same one recorded earlier, not a regression.

Still honest about scope. This is the hosting CONTROL LAYER: scheduling, leasing, binding, metering, tenant-owned storage. It is not a deployment — no process supervisor, no real cell process, no billing integration. Those are operational build-out, and I would rather ship a correct control layer with its invariants proven than a half-running system whose isolation nobody has tested.

WS-8 / §17 — what the spec actually asks commerce to do here

The owner was right that WS-8 is code, not a blocker, and reading §17 changed what that code is. §17 opens: "Commerce depends on the generic managed Dina runtime but does not define it. The following requirements are vertical integration constraints." Building a multi-tenant control plane, hosted runner supervisor and metering system inside the commerce pack is precisely what that sentence forbids — it would put the hosting product inside a vertical.

So the honest reading is that WS-8's four rows are the GENERIC runtime's work, and what commerce owes is the one constraint it can enforce and prove: §17.4's claim binding.

The audit. §17.4 requires every claim bound to seven things. Mapped against the guard: install ID ✓ (envelope-to-lane match), paired instance identity ✓ (install.deviceDid plus auth), exact lane ✓ (server-forced, client runner_filter ignored, exact-match-only selection), capability ✓ (consent map) and manifest CID ✓ (divergence admitted only through a live §9.13 drain authorization), claim token ✓ (claim_id CAS), authority snapshot ✓ (scope hash + config revision, both terminalizing). Tenant is structural: §17.1 keeps the single-writer Home Node model authoritative per tenant, so a tenant boundary is a whole Core with its own encrypted store and there is no tenant column to get wrong.

What was missing was not a check but a PROOF. The suite covered install isolation inside one database; nothing exercised the threat §17.4 actually names — "a vendor-wide identity alone cannot claim any tenant's task". Now four tests stand up TWO tenants as two encrypted stores with their own install repositories and workflow queues, and drive a single vendor device identity across them:

  • it MULTIPLEXES where paired into both — which §17.4 permits and is the point of hosting;

C7-07 — schema re-consent, where I was half right and the exclusion was wrong

A reviewer disagreement with a decision I made in an earlier round. Neither position was right as stated, and the resolution is a third thing.

I had excluded params_schema and result_schema from widening detection outright, reasoning that §9.13 makes MINOR "strictly additive" and the drain mechanism absorbs the ordinary minor release — so prompting on every schema tweak would train an owner to tap through the prompts that matter. Codex said both schemas sit inside scopeHashInput, and a changed per-capability scope hash requires re-consent.

Codex is right about the principle and I was right about the cost. Draining and consent are not substitutes: the drain governs tasks ALREADY created against their pinned schemas, while consent governs what the install may be asked to do NEXT. Excluding the field let an install adopt a new data contract — new required inputs, a different result shape — under an approval the owner gave for a different one.

The resolution: rank it, do not exclude it. The distinction §9.13 actually draws is not "changed" versus "unchanged" but ADDITIVE versus not — "new optional fields only". So a strictly additive change is the ordinary minor the drain exists for and passes silently; anything else is a new contract and asks. That satisfies the principle without the prompt fatigue, and it makes the rule the same shape as the four other ranked fields.

schemaWidened FAILS CLOSED, matching the module's stated philosophy ("known and ranked, or unknown and refused").
